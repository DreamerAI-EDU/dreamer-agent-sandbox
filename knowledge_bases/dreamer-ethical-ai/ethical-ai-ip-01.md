# Copyright and AI: Be an Original Creator, Not a Copycat

## Design Phase: Making Things You Can Be Proud to Own

You are about to create real things — posters, scripts, videos, games. Before you publish anything, you need to understand one rule that runs the whole creative world: **who owns what?**

### Hook: The Stolen Drawing

Ask students: "If you drew a beautiful picture, how would you feel if someone took it, put their name on it, and sold it?"

That feeling — that's what copyright protects. Copyright means "the right to copy." When someone creates something — a song, a story, a video, a drawing — they own it. Taking it without permission is taking their work, even if copying is technically easy.

### The Three Rules of Clean Creation

| Rule | What It Means | How To Follow It |
|---|---|---|
| **Create, don't copy** | Use AI to make new things, not to imitate existing characters, songs, or brands | "A robot explorer in space" ✅ / "Mickey Mouse in space" ❌ |
| **Use royalty-free materials** | Some creators share their work free for others to use | Search "royalty-free music / images" and check the license |
| **Credit your sources** | Say where things came from | Add a credits line: "Music: royalty-free from ___; images: AI-generated + my own edits" |

### The AI Twist: Who Owns AI Work?

Here is the puzzle this generation of students will grow up with: if AI draws a picture from your prompt, who owns it — you, the AI company, or nobody? Different countries are deciding this right now, and the answers differ. Two safe habits while the world figures it out:

1. **Add your own work** — edit, combine, overlay your own text and design. The more of *you* in it, the stronger your claim
2. **Never pass AI output off as purely hand-made** — and never pass copied work off as AI-made. Honesty about process is part of being a creator

### Activity: The Copyright Audit

**Scenario:** A teammate made a 45-second video containing: a famous pop song as background music, three images found on Google Images, one AI-generated background, and their own voiceover.

**Your Task:**

1. Audit each element: OK to use, or copyright risk?
2. For every risky element, propose a fix (royalty-free replacement, AI generation, self-made)
3. Write the credits list for the fixed version

### Deliverable: Creator's Pledge

**Format:** a credits block of exactly 5 lines, pasted where your project ends — video description, last slide, or bottom of the poster.

**Template:**

```
Title: [project name]
Made by me: [what you created yourself — e.g., script, voiceover, text overlays]
Made with AI: [what AI generated — e.g., two background images, Canva Magic Media]
Materials used with permission: [e.g., royalty-free track from ___]
One thing I learned about ownership: [one sentence]
```

**Filled example:**

```
Title: Save Our Ocean
Made by me: script, voiceover, all text overlays and transitions
Made with AI: two background images (Canva Magic Media)
Materials used with permission: "Waves" royalty-free track, YouTube Audio Library
One thing I learned about ownership: editing AI images myself makes the work more mine.
```

**Pass criteria (teacher checks):** all 5 lines present; at least one non-AI element listed under "Made by me"; every external material has a source named.

### Teacher Notes

- Design phase: students are making publishable work, so ownership rules arrive exactly when needed
- Cross-reference: thinking-critical (auditing materials), communication-representation (credits as professional practice)
- The "AI ownership" question has no settled answer — present it as an open question, not a gap in teaching
- Tags in play: transparency, accountability
- Practical tip: demonstrate one royalty-free search live — the skill is in knowing where to look

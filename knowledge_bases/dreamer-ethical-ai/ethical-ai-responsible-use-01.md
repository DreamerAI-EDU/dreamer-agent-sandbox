# Responsible Use: When NOT to Use AI

## Deliver Phase: The Hardest Skill Is Choosing Not To

You have learned what AI can do, how to prompt it, and how to check it. The final skill is the one most adults haven't mastered: **deciding when using AI is the wrong move — even when it would work.**

### Four Situations Where "AI Can" ≠ "AI Should"

| Situation | The Temptation | Why It's Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Homework meant to train you** | "AI帮我做功課，快好多" | The homework was never the point — the practice was. Outsourcing the reps means you arrive at the exam untrained |
| **Decisions about people** | Let AI rank classmates, friends, "who is trustworthy" | AI judgment about humans carries baked-in bias; using it on real people amplifies unfairness |
| **Pretending AI work is yours** | Submit AI output unchanged under your name | This is the honesty line: assistance is fine, substitution is not |
| **Emotional emergencies** | Asking AI what to do when a friend is in crisis | AI is not a counsellor; a real person (parent, teacher, hotline) must be told |

### The Effort Test

Before using AI on any task, ask: **"What am I supposed to be learning here — and will I still learn it?"**

- Using AI to check your essay's grammar after you wrote it → the reps were yours ✅
- Using AI to write the essay → the reps went to the machine ❌
- Using AI to brainstorm five ideas, then picking and building your own → your judgment did the work ✅
- Copying the first answer without reading it → you weren't in the room ❌

### Case Study: The Exam Hall Has No AI

Ka Yan used AI for every maths practice all term — every worksheet perfect, every answer correct. Then came the in-class test: no devices, 45 minutes, same type of problems. She scored 41%. The AI work was real; her skill wasn't.

**Discussion questions:**

1. Where exactly did things go wrong — the first time she used AI, or *how* she used it?
2. Steel-man the other side: "In real jobs, AI tools will be allowed — why train without them?" Build the strongest version of that argument, then find where it breaks.
3. Which skills must exist without AI (exam halls, live discussion, judging whether AI output is even right)? Which can safely be AI-assisted?

> Expect older students to push back: "Couldn't the artist just use AI to sketch live in the meeting?" Welcome the challenge — the goal is not proving "AI bad." The goal is the Effort Test: knowing which abilities must survive without the machine. And if someone argues future workplaces allow AI everywhere, ask one question: who checks the AI's work? Checking requires the very skill that was outsourced.

### Activity: Build the Class Charter

**Your Task:** In groups, draft a 6-8 line "AI Use Charter" for your class:

1. Two rules for when AI use is encouraged
2. Two rules for when AI use is banned
3. One rule about honesty (how to declare AI help)
4. One rule about people (what AI never decides)

Groups then debate and merge into one charter the whole class signs. Test every proposed rule against a real scenario — a rule that can't survive a concrete example gets rewritten.

### Deliverable: Personal Position

Write 200-300 words: "My personal line for AI use in schoolwork." Reference at least one charter rule you argued for or against, and one real situation from your own week where you applied the Effort Test.

### Teacher Notes

- Deliver phase capstone for the ethics thread — students leave with a self-authored rule, not a memorised one
- Cross-reference: thinking-critical (the Effort Test), self-management-reflection (personal position)
- The charter works best when students genuinely disagree — facilitate, don't settle
- Tags in play: accountability, transparency, consent
- Platform note: Dreamer AI itself follows these rules — it helps students think, it does not do their thinking. Students can and should hold the platform to the same charter

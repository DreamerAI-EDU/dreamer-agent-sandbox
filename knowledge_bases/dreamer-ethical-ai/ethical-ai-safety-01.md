# AI Safety: Hallucinations, Deepfakes, and Misinformation

## Discover Phase: Learning to Verify, Not Just Believe

You already know AI can write essays, draw pictures, and answer questions. Now discover its shadow side: **AI can be confidently wrong — and it can be used to fake reality itself.**

### Three Ways AI Breaks Trust

| Failure | What Happens | Real-World Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hallucination** | AI invents facts that sound true | A student asks for a book quote; the AI writes a perfect quote from a book chapter that does not exist |
| **Deepfake** | AI generates fake images, voices, or videos of real people | A video of a celebrity "endorsing" a product they never heard of; a cloned voice calling a parent asking for money |
| **Misinformation at scale** | AI makes it cheap to produce thousands of convincing fake posts | Fake news spread during elections or emergencies, written faster than humans can fact-check |

The pattern: none of these require the AI to be "evil." A prediction machine that always sounds confident is dangerous precisely because it sounds confident.

### Case Study: The Fake That Fooled a Company

In 2024, a finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with what looked and sounded like the company's CFO and colleagues. Every face on the call was an AI deepfake. The worker transferred HK$200 million. Lesson: "I saw it with my own eyes" no longer means what it used to.

### The Verification Habit: SIFT

Before believing or sharing anything AI-produced or suspicious online:

1. **Stop** — don't forward it yet
2. **Investigate the source** — who published this? Do they exist?
3. **Find better coverage** — do two independent reliable sources say the same?
4. **Trace the original** — can you find where the claim actually started?

### Activity: Fact-Check Report

**Scenario:** Your video project includes one factual claim (e.g., "Hong Kong sends over 1 million tonnes of waste to landfills every year").

**Your Task:**

1. Pick one fact from your own project work
2. Verify it using a search engine and at least one official source (government department, university, established news outlet)
3. Write a short report: the claim, what you found, and your verdict — confirmed, wrong, or "can't verify"

Example verdict: "My claim was wrong. The real number is higher. I need to fix the data in my video before publishing."

### Discussion: Who Is Responsible?

If an AI writes something false and a student submits it as homework — who is responsible? The AI company? The student? The teacher who didn't check? There is no easy answer, and that is the point: **accountability stays with humans**, which is why verification is a skill, not a feature.

### Teacher Notes

- Discover phase: students move from "AI can make mistakes" to building a personal verification habit
- Cross-reference: thinking-critical (evaluating claims), research-information-literacy (source checking)
- Hong Kong deepfake case grounds the discussion locally — students remember local stories
- Tags in play: safety, transparency, accountability
- Sensitive handling: if a student reports receiving a suspicious voice/video message, escalate to parents — treat as a real safety issue, not a classroom exercise
